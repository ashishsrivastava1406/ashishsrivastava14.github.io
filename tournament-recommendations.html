Tournament Recommendations

A recommendation engine built on three separate persona logics rather than one model, including a route that inferred tournament affinity from a player's ring-game spend. It raised revenue per user and did not move the participation it was built to move.

On this page

  1. The problem
  2. What I decided, and what I turned down
  3. What happened
  4. What I learned

The problem

Phase 1 sorted the lobby by segment. It did not answer the narrower question: for this player, right now, which specific tournaments should appear.

The commercial target was ring-only players — already paying customers who had never entered a tournament. The objective was to raise tournaments played per player and move ring-only players into playing both.

The difficulty is that the three groups differ in what data exists about them, not only in what they prefer. Tournament players have history. New users have nothing. Ring-only players have spend data from a different product.

What I decided, and what I turned down

Three separate logics rather than one ranking system. A single model would have been fitted on tournament history that two of the three groups did not have.

Deterministic rules rather than a learned model. Every input is a named, configurable threshold rather than a weight, so the output is explainable when it looks wrong and adjustable without retraining.

Infer tournament affinity from ring-game spend. For ring-only players the engine takes the median entry fee paid per game over a recent window, maps it to an entry fee band, and recommends tournaments in that band starting within the hour. The signal is what the player already spends rather than what similar players did.

Cover formats rather than optimise within one. Ring-only players get one deals, one points and one series tournament. A player who has never entered one does not know which format suits them.

Break ties on affordability. Where tournaments rank equally, the second tie-break is the smallest gap between wallet balance and entry fee. Prize pool ranks third.

Suppress the section below three qualifying tournaments rather than padding it. One irrelevant recommendation costs more than an absent section.

What happened

Revenue moved, participation did not. Tournament NGR per user rose 7.3%, tournament ARPU 7%, ring ARPU 4%. Tournaments per user was flat. The share of players playing tournaments was flat.

The two cohorts diverged sharply. Among players who had crossed the tournament-player threshold, adoption of recommendations rose about 40%, tournament ARPU 20%, and average ring entry fee 16%. Among those below it, adoption rose about 3% and tournament ARPU fell.

Against the stated objective this is a miss. The engine was built to increase tournaments played and convert ring-only players. It did neither. It directed players who already played tournaments toward more expensive ones.

What I learned

It monetised familiarity rather than creating it. Recommendation quality helps most where preference already exists, which is where the commercial upside is smallest. The ring-game inference was the interesting part of the design and it did not produce the behaviour it was built for.

Low adoption and low coverage are indistinguishable here. The specification anticipated that recommendation data might not generate for most players, and the analysis reports adoption. A 3% lift could mean recommendations were seen and ignored, or rarely generated at all. Those need different responses, and the split should have been read out alongside the primary metrics rather than left to inference.

Matching is not the constraint for a first conversion. Inferring the right entry fee band does not address why a ring player has never entered a tournament. That is comprehension and confidence, which a ranking system cannot supply.
