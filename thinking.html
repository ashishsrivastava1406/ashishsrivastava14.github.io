Thinking

Five things the case studies taught me, rather than five things I believe in general.

Instrument before intervening

The tournament lobby was described as dated. The loss was in the sort and category tab logic. Complaints report an experience rather than diagnose it, and the gap between what people say is wrong and what the funnel says is wrong is usually where the work is.

Start from the constraint

Regulation, inventory that expires, prize pools that run at a loss, and operations teams who have to configure the thing at four in the afternoon are not context around the product problem. They are the product problem, and a solution that ignores them is a solution to a different question.

Make the experiment able to disprove you

The lobby test ran three paths rather than two so the expensive half of the build could be measured on its own. It showed that half contributed nothing to the primary metric. An experiment that can only confirm the team's preferred answer is a launch with extra steps.

Measure what a user stopped doing

Every success metric on the lobby work pointed at tournaments. None measured what a player gave up to play one, and the substitution only surfaced because ring rake happened to sit on the guardrail list. Inside a product with competing surfaces, that belongs in the primary metric set.

Volume and value are different problems

Three separate experiments moved revenue per user and left frequency flat. The one that moved participation did it by leaving the surface entirely and meeting players where they already were. Recognising which of the two a piece of work can actually move prevents claiming the wrong win.
